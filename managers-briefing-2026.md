# PART ONE — FOR EVERYONE
## Your Mandate as a Leader in This Business

---

## The Short Version of Where We're Going

You've heard the numbers. Ten years of growth, doubling in the last 2.5 years, 160 people, $70–100M in revenue. That growth happened because this team gave a damn about doing good work. That hasn't changed.

What has changed is the scale. We're a different sized business now — and the way we operated to get here won't get us to where we're going next. By 2029 we're aiming at $140–180M. That's achievable. But it requires us to operate differently.

The three things the business needs to do:

| Objective | What it means for this room |
|---|---|
| **Hunting on the Front Foot** | Sales owns the pipeline. Everyone else delivers on what's sold. |
| **First Pass Business** | Touch it once. Engineering releases right. Production builds right. Customer receives right. |
| **Increase Throughput** | More output from the same people — by removing the friction that wastes capacity. |

**The leverage point is First Pass Business.** Fix that and the other two improve automatically. Every hour we spend on rework is an hour we're not building the next job.

---

## The Problem We're Solving — Together

There is a cascade failure running through our value stream right now. It starts in Engineering and amplifies through every downstream function. Every person in this room is affected by it — and every person in this room is part of fixing it.

```
Engineering releases late and incomplete
            ↓
Fabrication cannot close on time
            ↓
Operations costing and allocation cannot keep up
            ↓
Pressure floods the organisation → corners cut → errors → rework → repeat
```

This is not a people problem. Our people are good. It is a **systems and process** problem. The fix is also systems and process — and that's what we're here to talk about.

The data from our own quality system is clear:
- **4,964 repair actions** in 18 months — growing at +72% while the business grew at a lower rate
- **1 in 3 defects** is something simply missing — a BOM or checklist failure, preventable at the desk
- **392 defects reached the customer** — in aviation refuelling equipment, that's a serious risk
- **98% of repair actions have no root cause recorded** — we're fixing problems but not learning from them

We know what the problems are. We know where they start. We know what to do. This briefing is about making sure every leader in this business knows their part in fixing it.

---

## What Ownership Looks Like Here

The business has grown to a point where the CEO cannot be — and should not be — the answer to everything. That's not how scalable businesses work. Scalable businesses have leaders who own their domains.

**What ownership means in practice:**

**You know what's happening in your area before anyone else does.** Not because someone told you — because you're watching it. When something is wrong on your floor, in your team, in your numbers, you're the first person who knows — not the last.

**You produce your monthly KPI report — you don't receive it.** Your numbers are your responsibility to understand, explain, and act on. When a KPI is red, the question isn't "why is it red?" — it's "what have you already done about it?"

**You make decisions within your domain.** Decision rights are being made explicit for each role. If a decision is yours to make, make it. If you need to consult, consult quickly and decide. If you need to escalate, escalate with a recommendation — not just a problem.

**You lead your team through pressure, not around it.** When volume spikes or deadlines tighten, that's the moment standards matter most. Your job is to hold the standard when it's inconvenient — because your team takes its cue from you.

> *"Your team is watching what you do when it gets hard. That's when culture is made."*

Three things that apply to every leader in this business, regardless of function:

1. **Engineering release checklist** — no package leaves engineering without it complete. If you are downstream of engineering, you support this, not circumvent it.

# PART TWO — ENGINEERING
## Engineering Manager · Senior Mechanical Engineer · Senior Electrical Engineer

---

## The Honest Picture

Engineering is the constraint right now. The data is unambiguous about that. This is not blame — it is direction. Fixing it is the most valuable contribution the engineering team can make to this business in the next 12 months.

The three root causes are specific, and each one has a specific fix:

---

## Root Cause 1 — Schedule Has No Teeth → Fix: Weekly Throughput Review

**What changes:**
- Weekly engineering throughput review is established and never cancelled. Jobs in this week, jobs released this week, jobs at risk and why.
- Engineering Manager owns the queue. That means flagging capacity constraints **before** jobs go late — not after.
- We start building an actuals database by job type. Every completed job records actual hours vs. estimate. Within 6 months our estimates will be grounded in reality.
- Jobs are sequenced by downstream impact — a late aviation refueller that holds up fabrication takes priority over intake order.

**What this looks like on Monday:** Engineering Manager runs the first throughput review. List every open job. Mark everything that has a risk of missing its release date. Name the reason. Assign a mitigation. That's the baseline.

**Your KPI:** Engineering on-time release rate — target 85% at 6 months, 95% at 12 months.

---

## Root Cause 2 — BOMs Released Incomplete → Fix: This Week

This is the fastest win available in this entire strategy. The following items appear across multiple jobs in our repair data. They are missing from standard BOMs. Add them now and they never appear in a repair action again:

**Add to every relevant standard BOM before Friday:**

Spare wheel | Ladder | Fuel circuit plate/label | Control panel nutserts | 5th wheel VASS label | Wheel nut indicators | Pressure vessel door seal | Weighbridge docket | Filter vessel elements | Build checklist sign-off

**Estimated time: one hour. Benefit: dozens of repair actions eliminated immediately.**

**What changes ongoing:**
- BOM sign-off is a mandatory step in the release checklist — Engineering Manager sign-off that every line item is verified, quantities confirmed, part numbers resolved.
- When a repair action originates from a missing BOM item, the BOM is updated the same day. No exceptions.

**Your KPI:** BOM-originated production stops — trending to zero within 12 months.

---

## Root Cause 3 — No Quality Gate Before Release → Fix: Engineering Release Gate (30 Days)

**The release checklist — mandatory before any package leaves engineering:**

- [ ] SolidWorks interference detection completed at sub-assembly level
- [ ] Senior engineer spatial review completed on complex jobs (aviation refuellers, dispensers)
- [ ] BOM complete — every line item verified, quantities confirmed, part numbers resolved
- [ ] Peer drawing review signed off
- [ ] Engineering lead sign-off

**No package released without every box checked.** This applies under time pressure — especially under time pressure. The checklist exists for exactly that moment.

**Implementation:**
- Weeks 1–4: Write the checklist with the team. Align on what "complete" means for each step. Activate SolidWorks interference detection.
- Weeks 4–12: Every job through the gate. Track stops caused by incomplete packages.
- Months 3–6: Schedule accuracy improving as estimates are informed by real data.
- Months 6–12: On-time release at 85%+. Floor stops being surprised by engineering.

**On SolidWorks:** Whole-vehicle clash detection on large complex assemblies (aviation refuellers) is less reliable than sub-assembly level checks. The process is: interference check at sub-assembly level + senior engineer spatial review on complex jobs before release. This is the engineering team's call to make and document.

# PART THREE — OPERATIONS
## Operations Manager

---

## Your Place in the Picture

Operations owns costing and allocation — the step that converts a completed build into a closed job and a correct invoice. When this is slow, the consequences run in two directions: cash flow delays upstream and production plan failures downstream. Every job that sits in the costing queue is a job not yet closed, not yet invoiced, and not yet informing the next allocation decision.

The costing and allocation process is a real constraint on throughput. Engineering is the root cause of the cascade — but Operations is where the pressure of late engineering is most visibly felt, and where the fix must be most operationally practical.

**The target: costing turnaround reduced 40% within 6 months.**

---

## What Changes

**Step 1 — Map the process (this month)**

Trace a typical job from fabrication close to costing complete and mark every point where it pauses and why. The goal is to distinguish between:
- A **workflow problem** — the process has unnecessary steps, handoffs, or approval loops
- A **capacity problem** — the volume has outgrown the headcount available to do the work
- An **information problem** — costing can't start because the job information from upstream is incomplete

Bring the process map to the next monthly review. That's the basis for the improvement plan.

**Step 2 — Define what Operations needs from upstream to start costing immediately**

If jobs arrive with incomplete hours, missing job cards, or unclear BOM actuals, costing waits. The engineering release gate addresses package quality — but Operations must define exactly what a "costable job" looks like the moment it arrives. Document it. Share it with Engineering and Production.

**Step 3 — Assess systems (within 6 months)**

At current volume, manual costing processes may be at their limit. Assess whether a job costing or ERP system would materially improve turnaround speed and accuracy. Bring a recommendation to the CEO within 6 months — not a shortlist, a recommendation with a preferred option and rationale.

---

## Your Role in the Ownership Culture

Operations sits at the intersection of production, finance, and planning. When allocation decisions are wrong or slow, every other function feels it. That cross-functional visibility is a responsibility — you are often the first person who can see when the build plan and the resourcing reality are diverging.

**What ownership looks like for Operations:**
- When you can see a costing backlog building — surface it and propose the fix before being asked
- Monthly BSC operational perspective produced by Operations, not received by Operations
- Allocation decisions within your authority are made and communicated quickly — not held pending perfect information

**The shift:** from processing jobs → to actively managing the throughput of the operation.

# PART FOUR — FINANCE
## Finance Manager

---

## Your Role in the Business

Finance's role in this business is not costing — that sits with Operations. Finance's mandate is the financial integrity and visibility of the operation: accurate job time reporting, AP and AR management, and the monthly financial narrative that tells leadership whether the business is performing as expected.

Done well, Finance is an early warning system. Done poorly, Finance is a reporting function that confirms what everyone already suspects.

---

## Job Time Reporting — a Strategic Asset

Finance tracks actual job hours. Right now that data is used for reporting. It should also be used for improvement.

**What changes:**
- Actual hours by job type are shared with Engineering Manager monthly — this is the actuals database that makes future estimates more accurate
- Variance reports (actual vs estimated hours) are produced and presented, not just filed
- When a job type is consistently running over estimate, Finance flags it — it's a signal of either a scoping problem in Engineering or a production problem on the floor

**System consideration:** If job time monitoring is currently manual, the volume of work at 160 people makes this worth reviewing. A proper time and job tracking system — even a lightweight one — would improve both the accuracy and the speed of this data. Bring a view on this within 6 months.

---

## AP and AR — Cash is the Engine

At $70–100M revenue and growing, the timing of AP and AR is a cash flow lever, not just an admin function.

**AR:** Customer invoices go out promptly when jobs close. A job that sits in the costing queue is a job not yet invoiced. The costing turnaround improvement in Operations directly improves AR timing — Finance and Operations are connected here.

**AP:** Supplier payment terms are managed actively. Early payment discounts are taken where they make sense. Overdue supplier relationships are flagged before they become supply chain problems.

---

## Monthly Finance Reports — Own the Narrative

The monthly finance report is not a spreadsheet sent to leadership. It is Finance's interpretation of what the numbers mean for the business — produced by Finance, presented by Finance, owned by Finance.

**What this looks like:**
- Revenue per head trending — is throughput improving or declining per person?
- Margin by job type — are some product types consistently under-performing?
- Cash position and forecast — where are the next 90 days heading?
- BSC financial perspective — produced by Finance for the monthly review

**The shift:** from distributing numbers → to interpreting them and recommending action.
